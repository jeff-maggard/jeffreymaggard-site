Miyazaki · Fall 2003

Peace Studies Seminar on Cambodia

I founded the Peace Studies Club, led its campaign for an anti-trafficking NGO in Cambodia, co-created a seminar on Cambodia, and took the students to meet the NGO and the people they supported.

My role
Founded the Peace Studies Club and served as its faculty advisor. Co-created the seminar. Led the student trip to Phnom Penh and Siem Reap. Helped raise more than $3,000 for an NGO.

The purpose of the seminar

To teach peace studies and conflict resolution to Miyazaki International University students and members of the local Miyazaki community.

Lead-up to the seminar

In January 2003, I visited Siem Reap and met a man who worked with the Vulnerable Children’s Assistance Organization (VCAO), a UNHCR program that stops children from being sold into slavery and prostitution. This is what VCAO does:

The problem VCAO fights

A $100 promise

Traffickers offer poor families about $100 and a factory job for a child, then sell the child. The parents are tricked; they don’t realize what is happening.

VCAO prevention

Village workshops

In two-day conflict resolution workshops, children and adults learn to identify their problems and plan solutions. Most problems trace back to poverty. The solution: move children at risk to a safe house, out of the traffickers’ reach.

VCAO protection

A guarded safe house

Eleven girls lived in his family home under armed guard, safe from traffickers.

Life at the safe house in Siem Reap. Krama are long silk scarves; VCAO sells them to fund its work.

The role of the Peace Studies Club

In March 2003, in Miyazaki, I founded the Peace Studies Club with a student who hoped to become an NGO worker. My colleague Dr. Kiyohiko Toyama encouraged me to start it before he left for Tokyo, newly elected to the House of Councillors, the upper house of Japan’s parliament.

From the start of the school year, the club ran a campaign for VCAO, and the Drama, Music and Dance Clubs joined it. All the money raised went to VCAO:

  1. Saturday markets

    I bought krama for $10 each, and the club sold them for $20 (¥2,000). Each sale doubled the money, which bought more krama. This went on for three years.

  2. A concert

    An evening concert in a concert hall, with ticket sales.

  3. A comedy show and play

    An evening show at the university, where we collected donations.

  4. Donation boxes

    Boxes in convenience and grocery stores across town.

The seminar

By June 2003, the campaign had built strong interest across Miyazaki. To build on it, I co-created the Peace Studies Seminar on Cambodia with Dr. Elizabeth Lokon. It ran for 15 weeks in the fall semester of 2003.

Mr. Ok Veth, the Cambodian Ambassador’s Counsellor, opened the seminar. Sessions by 11 MIU faculty and guest speakers covered Cambodian history and the Khmer Rouge, Buddhism, anthropology and the economy. An NGO spoke on landmine removal, and staff from Japan’s embassy in Phnom Penh presented their Official Development Assistance (ODA) work.

More than 200 students and community members from Miyazaki attended. Everyone who attended 12 or more sessions received a certificate.

Dr. Toyama spoke at the closing session and thanked the students and the community for their work.

The Peace Studies Club trip

In February 2004, I led the students to Cambodia. At VCAO headquarters in Phnom Penh, the Peace Studies Club gave VCAO more than $3,000, everything it had raised. The staff showed us their projects. In Siem Reap, the students met the girls they had been supporting, and we bought more krama to sell at home.

The impact

  • The girls stayed in the safe house and built their weaving skills. Once skilled, they took their looms home and kept weaving to support their families. The safe house still runs today.
  • More than 20 years later, the university still teaches peace and conflict studies: a 15-week course led by a political science professor.
  • Dr. Jeong-Pyo Hong’s seminar lecture, “Sino-Cambodian Relations: from the Chinese Perspective,” was cited as a publication in a 2004 Korea Institute for International Economic Policy report.

Peace Studies Club student outcomes

  • The student who co-founded the club became an NGO worker. She has worked in Cambodia, Thailand, Bangladesh, India and Laos.
  • In 2003, I trained club members to do research and co-wrote a paper with them, “Young Japanese people’s views on civic (dis)engagement.” I led them to Tokyo, where we presented it together at the 2nd Peace as a Global Language Conference. On the same trip, the students met Dr. Toyama at the National Diet Building. He was a sitting member of the upper house at the time. He praised their work and encouraged them to keep going.
  • In 2004, I led the students to Kyoto, where we presented our co-written research, “Developing social responsibility and IC skills,” at the 3rd Peace as a Global Language Conference.
