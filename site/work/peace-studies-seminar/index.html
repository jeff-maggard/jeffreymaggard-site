Miyazaki · Fall 2003

Peace Studies Seminar on Cambodia

I co-created a peace studies seminar on Cambodia, led a student fundraising campaign for a Cambodian NGO, and took the students to meet the girls they supported.

My role
Co-created the seminar. Peace Studies Club faculty advisor and leader of the student trip to Phnom Penh and Siem Reap.

The purpose

To teach peace studies and conflict resolution, to help students and the Miyazaki community understand Cambodia’s recovery and peacebuilding, and to connect them with the people doing that work.

How it started

In 2003 I met my friend Nara in Siem Reap. His family had given his grandparents’ home to the Vulnerable Children’s Assistance Organization (VCAO), a UNHCR program. Eleven girls lived there, kept safe from people who prey on poor families.

Upstairs, in a traditional wooden house on stilts, a teacher taught the girls to read and write, grow food and raise chickens and livestock. Downstairs, they had just begun weaving silk krama on looms from another NGO. VCAO sells krama in its shops to fund its programs. I bought the very first ones the girls made, at $10 each, and carried them back to Japan.

In Miyazaki, I founded the Peace Studies Club with a student who wanted to become an NGO worker. I was teaching political science and conflict resolution; the club put those ideas to work.

How VCAO builds peace

VCAO runs two-day conflict resolution workshops in villages. Children and adults meet in separate groups. On day one, they name their problems. On day two, they work out solutions.

Many participants cannot read or write, so they draw their challenges: food, water, clothing, shelter, education and health. Almost every drawing leads back to poverty.

Raising funds

Before the seminar, the students turned a box of scarves into a citywide campaign. MIC’s Drama, Music, Dance and Peace Studies Clubs worked on it together.

  1. Saturday markets

    Students sold the krama for about ¥2,000 ($20) each, double what I paid. I sent the money to Nara and VCAO, bought every scarf they had, then 30 more, and the students held another market.

  2. A concert

    Students staged an evening concert in a city concert hall and sold tickets.

  3. A comedy show and play

    An evening show at the university drew donations from students, faculty, staff and guests.

  4. Donation boxes

    Students placed boxes in convenience stores and grocery stores around town.

By the time the seminar opened, Miyazaki already knew the cause.

The seminar

I co-created the Peace Studies Seminar on Cambodia with Dr. Elizabeth Lokon. The Cambodian Ambassador’s Counsellor, embassy staff from Japan, 11 MIC faculty, plus NGO staff gave presentations.

The seminar ran for a full semester of 15 weekly sessions. More than 200 students and community members from Miyazaki attended. Everyone who attended nine or more sessions received a certificate.

The trip

In February 2004, I led the students to Cambodia. At VCAO headquarters in Phnom Penh, we handed the head of VCAO everything we had raised, and the staff showed us their projects. In Siem Reap, the students met the girls they had been supporting, and we bought more krama to sell at home.

The impact

$3,000raised (about ¥300,000)
3 yearsof support for the girls
4student clubs working together
  • The girls stayed in the house and kept building their weaving skills.
  • The student who co-founded the club became an NGO worker. She now works in Bangladesh, India and Laos.
  • The seminar still runs at the university today, led by a political science professor.

The club also presented its research:

  • Prepared students to present our paper, “Young Japanese people’s views on civic (dis)engagement,” at the 2nd Peace as a Global Language Conference in Tokyo (2003).
  • Co-presented our research results from “Developing social responsibility and IC skills” at the 3rd Peace as a Global Language Conference in Kyoto (2004).
