The purpose

To preserve folktales from Mongolia and Japan and share them across both countries, by training university students to collect the stories and perform them as kamishibai for K-12 students.

How it started

I wrote the grant and served as Principal Investigator. I built a team around the project. One team member, a former Vice President of the Asian Development Bank, helped the proposal win funding from UNESCO through the Asia/Pacific Cultural Centre for UNESCO (ACCU).

The program

More than 40 faculty and students took part at Akita International University and the University of the Humanities (Ulaanbaatar) to teach folklore and kamishibai theatre traditions to hundreds of K-12 students in Mongolia and Japan.

Kamishibai is a Japanese storytelling tradition in which a performer presents a sequence of illustrated paper panels on a small stage, sliding each scene across the frame in rhythm with the narration. The storyteller uses expressive vocal techniques, gestures, timing, and character voices reminiscent of traditional Japanese theatrical performance. In many ways, manga can be seen as its modern analogue: both rely on sequential images, visual pacing, and the interaction between images and narrative to tell a story.

  • Led the student team to Mongolia and trained University of the Humanities students and faculty in kamishibai methods.

The DVD holds the Japanese and Mongolian kamishibai stories as PowerPoint slideshows. It has videos on collecting the folktales from oral tellers. It also has a PPT with instructions on how to create kamishibai illustrations, and how to use traditional voice-acting methods.

The history of kamishibai

Kamishibai, or paper theater, has very old roots in the Heian period.

Heian-era picture scrolls (794–1185)

In Heian-era Kyoto, artists painted emakimono: illustrated handscrolls that joined text with pictures in sequence. The Tale of Genji scrolls are the best-known example. By the late Heian and Kamakura periods, Buddhist monks used picture scrolls to tell stories and moral lessons to people who could not read, pointing to each scene as they spoke.

Street kamishibai

Storytellers rode bicycles from street to street (1930s–1950s) with a small wooden stage, the butai. They clapped wooden hyoshigi sticks to call children, then slid one picture card after another through the frame as they told the story.

Kamishibai today

Teachers and librarians still use kamishibai. The pairing of pictures and voice holds an audience and helps young learners follow a story in sequence. Our program brought this tradition to students in Mongolia and Japan.
